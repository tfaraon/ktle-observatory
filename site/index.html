Kati Thanda–
Lake Eyre

Australia’s lowest point and largest salt lake, dry for most of its history and, when the rivers of its basin reach it, an inland sea for a while. This observatory follows its water from space, alongside its natural history, its catchment, its plants and animals, and the Aboriginal peoples whose country it is.

Weather

Wind drives the shallow water of a filled lake across its bed, piling it against one shore and raising waves, and it is one of the conditions the hydrodynamic scenarios are matched against. The Bureau of Meteorology stations around the lake report wind, temperature and rain every half hour.

How the site is kept current

Weather is refreshed hourly, and bird sightings, community observations, rainfall and river gauges daily. Satellite levels and the water extent are recomputed when a new SWOT pass is available. Each page states when its data were last fetched, and says so plainly when a source could not be reached.

Rainfall over the basin

Almost all the water that reaches Kati Thanda falls as rain far to the north-east, over the Queensland headwaters of the Diamantina and Cooper. This page follows that rain over the whole basin, day by day.

River flow

Rain that falls on the Queensland headwaters takes weeks or months to travel down the Channel Country, and only rarely reaches the lake. The river gauges still operating in the basin measure it on the way; many others have closed.

Observe and share

Every photograph of a plant or an animal taken around Kati Thanda can become a record for science. On iNaturalist, observers share what they see, a community of naturalists helps to identify it, and observations that the community confirms become research grade and are shared with the biodiversity databases used by researchers and land managers.

See every observation on iNaturalist

How to take part

  1. Install the iNaturalist app, or open inaturalist.org, and create a free account.
  2. Photograph a plant or an animal, from more than one angle if you can. The app records where and when.
  3. Upload the observation with your best guess of what it is, or with none at all.
  4. Other naturalists help to identify it. When enough of them agree, the observation becomes research grade.
  5. Join the Kati Thanda–Lake Eyre project to follow what others find.

Observe with care

Kati Thanda is Arabana country and a national park. Observe from the shore and the public tracks: the Arabana ask visitors not to walk or boat on the lake, where a footprint stays until the next big flood (ABC News, 2025), and park rules do not allow boating (National Parks and Wildlife Service SA).

The tracks to the lake cross pastoral stations, whose owners ask visitors to stay on the main tracks, close any gate they find closed, take their rubbish with them and leave the trees standing (Mitchell, 1991, in A Natural History of the Lake Eyre Region).

Keep well back from nesting birds. iNaturalist blurs the location of threatened species on its own, and you can choose to blur the location of any of your observations.
